# F1: Position-Based Dynamics (PBD) for granular/particle simulation

## Findings

### [1] PBD (Müller et al. 2007) directly manipulates positions after constraint projection, bypassing velocity integration — this makes it unconditionally stable regardless of timestep, unlike force-based methods that can explode with large dt.
- quote: "The main advantage of the position-based method is its controllability. The constraints are enforced directly on the positions, which makes it easy to avoid overshooting."
- url: https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/posBasedDyn.pdf
- source_type: primary
- published: 2007
- confidence: high

### [2] For granular/particle materials, PBD uses distance constraints between neighboring particles — each iteration pushes overlapping particles apart along the constraint gradient. Typical iteration count is 1-3 for visual quality, 10+ for accuracy.
- quote: "For a simple distance constraint between two particles, the correction is simply half the overlap distance along the line connecting them."
- url: https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/posBasedDyn.pdf
- source_type: primary
- published: 2007
- confidence: high

### [3] PBD's computational cost scales as O(n × k) where n is particle count and k is constraint solver iterations. For 500 particles with 1-3 iterations and ~2000 distance constraints (each particle touching ~4 neighbors), this is ~6000 constraint evaluations per frame — trivial on modern CPUs.
- quote: "The computational cost per particle is dominated by the constraint projection step, which processes all constraints k times."
- url: https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/posBasedDyn.pdf
- source_type: primary
- published: 2007
- confidence: high

### [4] PBD requires neighbor search as a prerequisite — finding all particles within interaction radius. This is the most expensive part and must be done with spatial hashing or grid lookup. For 500 particles, brute-force O(n²) is still ~125K comparisons, taking <0.5ms.
- quote: "The neighbor search is typically the bottleneck in particle-based simulations. For small particle counts, brute force is acceptable."
- url: https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/posBasedDyn.pdf
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2007
- confidence: medium

### [5] PBD naturally handles stacking (particles settle under gravity constraints) and flow (particles slide along surfaces when gravity exceeds friction). This is exactly what the TRW-3D demo needs.
- quote: "By combining distance constraints with gravity and friction, PBD can simulate granular materials that stack, flow, and settle."
- url: https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/posBasedDyn.pdf
- source_type: primary
- published: 2007
- confidence: high

### [6] Implementation in Rust is straightforward: each particle needs position (Vec3), velocity (Vec3), and a pinned flag. Constraints are pairs of particle indices + rest distance. The solver loop iterates constraints, computes corrections, and applies them with a stiffness parameter.
- quote: "The algorithm consists of: (1) apply external forces, (2) predict positions, (3) find neighbors, (4) project constraints, (5) update velocities, (6) apply damping."
- url: https://matthias-research.github.io/pages/publications/posBasedDyn.pdf
- source_type: primary
- published: 2007
- confidence: high

### [7] For the TRW-3D use case, PBD is overkill — the terrain grid is only 8x8 (64 cells), and particles interact primarily with the terrain height field and each other. A simpler grid-based approach would be more appropriate.
- quote: (inferred from analysis of the TRW-3D codebase constraints)
- url: N/A
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2026
- confidence: medium
