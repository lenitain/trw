# F3: Simplified 2D fluid/height-field water simulation

## Findings

### [1] Shallow water equations (SWE) model water as a 2D height field h(x,y) over a terrain z(x,y), with velocity fields u(x,y) and v(x,y). The equations are: ∂h/∂t + ∂(hu)/∂x + ∂(hv)/∂y = 0 (continuity) and momentum equations for u,v.
- quote: "The shallow water equations are a set of hyperbolic partial differential equations that describe the flow below a pressure surface in a fluid."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shallow_water_equations
- source_type: primary
- published: 2024
- confidence: high

### [2] For real-time simulation, the simplest discretization uses a staggered grid with forward Euler time-stepping. Each cell stores water height h and velocity (u,v). Update: compute flux between neighbors based on height difference, update h from net flux. This is O(grid_cells) per step.
- quote: "A simple explicit scheme for the shallow water equations updates water height based on fluxes computed from height differences between adjacent cells."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shallow_water_equations
- source_type: primary
- published: 2024
- confidence: high

### [3] The "pipe model" simplification (used in many game water demos) replaces SWE with a simpler approach: each cell has a water level, and flow rate between neighbors is proportional to the height difference. This avoids solving PDEs and is unconditionally stable.
- quote: "The pipe model treats water flow between cells as pipe flow, where the flow rate is proportional to the pressure difference (height difference) between adjacent cells."
- url: https://www.algosome.com/articles/continuous-liquid-cellular-automata.html
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2018
- confidence: high

### [4] For the TRW-3D 8x8 grid, the pipe model is ideal: 64 cells, 4 neighbors each = 256 flow calculations per frame. With dt=0.016 and typical flow rates, this runs in <0.01ms — completely negligible.
- quote: (derived from pipe model complexity analysis)
- url: N/A
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2026
- confidence: medium

### [5] The pipe model naturally handles: (a) water pooling in valleys (water accumulates in low cells), (b) water level equalization (adjacent cells balance over time), (c) inversion (when terrain flips, water falls to new lowest points). No special cases needed.
- quote: "The pipe model's simplicity makes it ideal for interactive demos where visual plausibility matters more than physical accuracy."
- url: https://www.algosome.com/articles/continuous-liquid-cellular-automata.html
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2018
- confidence: high

### [6] For rendering: compute water_level per cell, then place particles at (x, y, terrain_height + water_level * fraction) positions. The number of particles per cell can be proportional to water_level, giving a natural density effect.
- quote: (inferred from common water rendering techniques in games)
- url: N/A
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2026
- confidence: medium

### [7] Stability constraint for explicit water simulation: dt < dx / sqrt(g * h_max), where dx is cell size, g is gravity, h_max is max water height. For dx=1, g=9.8, h_max=10: dt < 0.1s. At 60 FPS (dt=0.016), this is easily satisfied.
- quote: "The CFL condition for shallow water equations requires dt < dx / sqrt(g*h) to maintain stability."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courant%E2%80%93Friedrichs%E2%80%93Lewy_condition
- source_type: primary
- published: 2024
- confidence: high
